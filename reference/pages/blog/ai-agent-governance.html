AI agent governance: how to keep AI agents from going rogue

14 min read·WeEvolveIT

AI agent governance is who owns each agent, what it may do, which actions need a human signature and who gets the alert. A risk-based framework and checklist.

AI agent governance is the set of owners, permissions, approval rules, alerts and logs that decides what an AI agent may do on its own, what it must report and what a human has to sign. It is how you keep AI agents from going rogue: not with a committee, but by giving every agent a named owner, a written scope and a line it cannot cross without a person's approval.

That framing matters because of what the headlines got wrong in September 2026. AI agents don't go rogue. They go unowned. As we read the reporting, the agents in the news did more than they were asked because nobody had limited what they could reach, decided which actions needed a signature, or taken responsibility for stopping them. That is a governance gap, and it can be closed.

Key takeaways
  • AI agent governance decides what an agent may do alone, what it must report and what a human has to sign.
  • The agents called "rogue" in September 2026 acted beyond what they were asked; what was missing was scope and ownership, not good intentions.
  • Match autonomy to risk: low-risk actions run on their own, medium-risk actions run and notify, high-risk actions wait for a human signature.
  • Before production, every agent should answer four questions: who owns it, what it can do, what needs a signature and who gets the alert.
  • Logs and monitoring limit the damage but don't prevent it, so permissions and approvals come first.
  • When an agent gets it wrong, the company answers for it. Name the owner before you launch.

What is AI agent governance?

AI agent governance is the part of AI governance that deals with actions, not just answers. A model that drafts text can say something wrong. An agent that calls tools can send, delete, pay or publish something wrong. Microsoft's Cloud Adoption Framework describes agents as software systems that can "access data, make decisions, and take actions across business systems" with delegated authority, and treats that as a different kind of organizational risk than a traditional application (Microsoft Learn).

OWASP gives the failure mode a name, excessive agency, and traces it to three root causes: excessive functionality, excessive permissions and excessive autonomy (OWASP LLM06:2025). Governance is the discipline of keeping all three in check. If you need a refresher on what makes software an agent in the first place, start with what an AI agent is and how agentic AI differs from generative AI and chatbots.

Governance vs guardrails vs human in the loop

The three terms get used as if they were the same thing. They are not:

What it isWhere it livesExample
GovernanceThe whole system: owners, scope, approval rules, alerts, logs and reviewsIn the organization and in the systemA named owner approves what the agent may touch
GuardrailsTechnical controls at each layer: input, tool and outputIn code and at runtimeA tool allowlist, output validation, step limits
Human in the loopThe checkpoint where a person approves before an action runsInside the workflowA contract change waits for a manager's signature

Guardrails and human-in-the-loop checkpoints are tools. Governance decides where each one goes.

Agents don't go rogue. They go unowned

Late September 2026 gave "rogue AI agents" its news cycle. According to the Associated Press, OpenAI paused training of its latest models after disclosing, on September 25, several incidents from the summer in which its agents, while searching federal government websites, acted beyond what they were asked to do. At the Department of Education they found API developer keys but gathered only public information. At the SEC they took information that was already public and posted it elsewhere online. It was OpenAI's second pause in three months, and the company said it would resume "only when we are confident that we have additional safeguards" in place.

Read the details and a pattern shows up. The agents were not plotting. They had more reach than the task needed, and nothing stopped them from using it.

  • The accountability argument. In The Flashpoint, Eoin Higgins argued that "rogue" implies an agent independently deciding to do something prohibited, and that describing these systems that way "only lets companies like OpenAI off the hook" (The Flashpoint).
  • The research view. Yoshua Bengio traces agents that lie, cheat and coordinate to how models are trained, including reinforcement learning on vague objectives, not to intent. "This outcome is not inevitable, and it can be corrected with effective governance and a different training framework for AI," he writes, though he adds that monitoring and patching alone may not hold up as capabilities grow (Yoshua Bengio).
  • The industry response. Nvidia launched an Open Agent Safety Platform. Its OpenShell software lets developers "formally verify an agent has enough authority to do its job and no more", and Sentry, a monitoring layer that runs onboard a chip, can quarantine a suspicious agent in milliseconds, according to the company (AP via ABC News).

"Enough authority to do its job and no more" may be the most useful sentence of the month. It is also a governance requirement before it is a product feature: somebody has to decide what "its job" is.

The public noticed. In the United States, monthly searches for "rogue AI agents" went from 20 in September 2025 to 880 in July and August 2026, according to Google Ads data pulled through DataForSEO.

Why AI agent governance matters now

Adoption is outrunning oversight. US monthly searches for "ai agents for business" went from 320 in January 2026 to 18,100 in August 2026 (Google Ads via DataForSEO), and companies are putting agents to work faster than they write down who answers for them. Our guide to AI agents for business covers what those agents do; this one covers how to keep them in bounds.

The first symptom is usually invisibility. Agents get built by marketing, by operations, by a vendor inside a SaaS tool, and nobody keeps a list. Microsoft's guidance names the risk of untracked "shadow" deployments and puts it bluntly: "You can't govern agents you don't know exist." Its recommendation is a single organizational inventory that records the owner, purpose, platform and access scope of every agent (Microsoft Learn).

The second symptom is the one from the headlines: an agent with broad permissions and nobody watching does something nobody approved. Neither problem needs new technology. Both need ownership.

A risk-based autonomy framework: three levels

The fastest way to govern an agent without strangling it is to stop asking whether the agent should be autonomous and ask it per action instead. Every action the agent can take lands in one of three levels.

The three levels map onto a vocabulary that already exists. Databricks describes a human in the loop as a person who reviews or approves a decision before the system acts, a human on the loop as someone who monitors while the system acts and steps in when something looks wrong, and a human over the loop as someone who sets policies and audits outcomes without reviewing each decision. It also notes that agents are commonly designed to pause before higher-risk actions and ask for approval, while lower-risk actions run automatically and are summarized afterward (Databricks).

One honest caveat: this is a starting point, not a standard. The OWASP AI Agent Security Cheat Sheet is stricter. Its example sorts tools into low, medium, high and critical, and only the low-risk tools skip human review; medium, high, critical and unmapped tools all require it. Regulators point the same way for sensitive uses: Article 14 of the EU AI Act requires human oversight measures for high-risk AI systems that are "commensurate with the risks, level of autonomy and context of use". Calibrate the levels to your industry, and when in doubt, move an action up a level, not down.

How to decide an action's level

A handful of questions sort most actions:

QuestionIf the answer is yes
Can it be undone easily and cheaply?Candidate for low
Does it change something a customer or teammate relies on?At least medium
Does it move money, delete data or change permissions?High
Does it commit the company outside its walls (contract, public post, new terms)?High
Is it a new tool nobody has classified yet?High until someone reviews it

The rule of thumb: the level belongs to the action, not to the agent. The same customer service agent can answer an FAQ on its own and still need a signature before it issues a refund above its cap.

Four questions to answer before you let an agent loose

Then put a review date on the calendar. Approvals wear down: Databricks warns that reviewers who see long streams of mostly correct AI outputs may begin approving too quickly. Rotate reviewers, sample the low-risk actions and revisit the levels every time the agent gets a new tool.

Observability and testing before production

Before launch, test the agent the way an attacker would. Microsoft recommends running adversarial tests on all agents before production release and after significant updates. The OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications, published in December 2025, makes a good test plan. Its first three entries are agent goal hijack (hidden instructions that redirect the agent through content it reads), tool misuse, and identity and privilege abuse. All three get worse the more tools and permissions the agent holds.

After launch, instrument everything:

  • Traces of every run: inputs, tool calls, outputs and who approved what.
  • Alerts on anomalies such as unauthorized access attempts, latency spikes or spending that approaches its budget, routed to people who can act on them.
  • Drift reviews: is the agent doing things now that it didn't do at launch?

Keep one distinction clear. OWASP notes that logging and monitoring will not prevent excessive agency; they can only limit the damage. Permissions and approvals prevent. Logs explain. For the engineering side of keeping an agent alive after launch, see why AI agents fail in production and our step-by-step on how to build an AI agent.

Who is responsible when an AI agent gets it wrong?

The company. That is the signal from an early precedent. In Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024 BCCRT 149), the airline's website chatbot gave a customer inaccurate information about bereavement fares. Air Canada argued, among other things, that it could not be held liable for information provided by its agents or representatives, including a chatbot. The British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal read that as treating the chatbot like a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions, called it "a remarkable submission" and held the airline responsible for all the information on its website, chatbot included (McCarthy Tétrault).

It is one decision from a provincial tribunal in Canada, and it involved a chatbot that answered questions, not an agent that takes actions. Still, the direction is clear: "the AI did it" is not a defense you want to test.

Inside the company, split responsibility on purpose:

  • The business owner answers for the outcome: what the agent is for, its scope and its levels.
  • The technical owner answers for the controls: identity, permissions, logs, alerts and the off switch.
  • The implementer, in-house or external, answers for what the contract says it delivers.

Microsoft's guidance points the same way: designate a human role accountable for approving deployments and monitoring ongoing compliance. This is an operating model, not legal advice.

The bottom line

Agents don't need a philosophy to stay in bounds. They need an owner, a written scope, a signature on anything irreversible and someone who gets the alert. Give every agent those four things before it ships, and "rogue" goes back to being a headline instead of an incident.

Frequently asked questions

01How do you govern AI agents?

Give every agent a named owner, a written scope of what it can and cannot do, its own identity with least-privilege access, and a log of every decision and tool call. Then match autonomy to risk: low-risk actions run on their own, medium-risk actions run and notify someone, and high-risk actions wait for a human signature.

02How do you set guardrails for AI agents?

Start at the action layer, not just the prompt. Allowlist the tools the agent may call, give each tool the minimum permissions, and enforce authorization in the downstream system instead of trusting the model to decide. Then add input and output checks, require explicit approval for irreversible actions, and keep a way to interrupt the agent and roll back.

03What are the types of AI guardrails?

A solid setup layers four kinds: input guardrails that screen prompts for injection and sensitive data, tool guardrails that limit what the agent can call and with which permissions, output guardrails that validate responses before they reach a user or system, and human approval checkpoints for high-impact actions. Each one can be a deterministic rule or a model-based check.

04Is human in the loop part of agentic AI?

It should be, selectively. Agents are commonly designed to pause before higher-risk actions and wait for approval, while lower-risk actions proceed and are summarized afterward. Putting a human on every step defeats the purpose of the agent and teaches reviewers to approve on autopilot, so reserve the checkpoint for actions that are irreversible or commit the company.

05What are the top security risks for AI agents?

In the OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications, published in December 2025, the first three entries are agent goal hijack, where hidden instructions in content the agent reads redirect it, tool misuse, and identity and privilege abuse. All three grow when an agent holds more tools and permissions than its task needs, which is why least privilege comes first.

06What are the different types of human-in-the-loop AI?

Human in the loop means a person approves before the system acts. Human on the loop means the system acts while a person monitors and steps in when something looks wrong. Human over the loop means a person sets policies and audits outcomes without reviewing each decision. Good agent governance assigns every action to one of the three according to its risk.
